# 1. History and Evolution

Payment cards grew out of the store accounts of the 1920s. In 1950 Diners Club issued the first card accepted by many unrelated businesses, and banks soon joined their cards into the networks that became Visa and Mastercard. Cards reached Europe and Croatia in the 1960s. They went online in the mid-1990s, once encryption made it safe to send card numbers over the internet. Since then, paying by card has become safer and easier to accept, and it now faces competition from direct bank payments and the planned digital euro.

## Buying on Trust (1920s–1940s)

Before there were payment cards, there was trust. Shops let regular customers **buy on account**: take the goods today, pay at the end of the month. The arrangement cost the shop twice over. It had to keep records of who owed what, and it carried the risk that some debts would never be paid.

In the 1920s, US department stores and oil companies began handing these customers cards to identify them at the counter. The best known was the **Charga-Plate**, introduced in 1928: a small metal plate, much like a dog tag, embossed with the customer's name and address. The clerk pressed it onto the sales slip to copy the details, and the store sent the bill later. Each card worked only at the company that issued it.

## One Card, Many Places (1950s)

In 1950, **Diners Club** took the store card a step further: one card for many businesses. Founded in New York, it started with a small group of unrelated restaurants. Diners Club paid the restaurant, then sent the cardholder a single bill each month, to be paid in full. A card that works this way is a **charge card**. American Express followed with its own in 1958.

The model spread because everyone involved came out ahead:

- **Customers** could pay without cash, in many places, and settle it all with one monthly bill.
- **Merchants** sold more and no longer had to track customer debts. The card company paid them and carried the risk that a customer would not pay.
- **The card company** earned from both sides: a yearly fee from cardholders, and a small share of each sale from merchants, who gave it up for the customers the card brought in.

Banks saw the opportunity. In 1958 Bank of America launched **BankAmericard**, the first widely successful **credit card**. Unlike a charge card, it offered **revolving credit**: cardholders could pay part of the bill each month, and the bank charged interest on the rest.

## The Networks Take Shape (1960s–1980s)

There was a limit to how far one bank could go: it could only sign up so many shops. So in the 1960s, banks began working together, joining into networks where a card issued by one bank was accepted by merchants signed up with another. BankAmericard grew into **Visa** (1976), while a rival network, formed in 1966, later became **Mastercard** (1979).

In the 1970s, payments went electronic. **Magnetic stripes** were added to cards, which held data that machines could read and send through the card networks. Payment approvals became fast and automated.

Meanwhile, cards were making their way to Europe. They arrived in the 1960s, and over time most of them became **debit cards**, which take the money directly from the cardholder's bank account. Croatia saw its first cards in the late 1960s.

## Cards Go Online (1990s)

The mid-1990s brought cards to the internet. The browser company Netscape created the security protocol **SSL**, which encrypted the connection between the browser and the website. Card numbers could now be sent safely over the internet.

**Payment gateways**, which appeared in 1995–96, connected websites to the card networks. A gateway took the card details entered on the website, sent them on for approval, and passed the answer back. With them, any online shop could accept card payments.

**PayPal** (1999) saved the customer's card details, so paying took only an email address and a password. The seller never touched the card data, which meant even private sellers could accept card payments.

By the end of the decade, online card payments had reached Europe.

## Securing the Payment (2000s)

As online shopping grew, so did fraud. Online, the card itself is never shown, only its details, so anyone who had stolen them could pay with someone else's card. Shops that saved card details became targets too, since a single break-in could expose millions of cards at once.

The card companies' first response was **3-D Secure**, launched by Visa in 2001, with Mastercard following soon after. It added an extra check to online payments: before a payment went through, the cardholder was sent to their bank's page to enter a password. Stolen card details alone were no longer enough, because the bank now confirmed that the real cardholder was paying.

The second came in 2004, when Visa, Mastercard and other card networks together published **PCI DSS**, the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard. It set security rules for every business that stored, processed or sent card data, such as protecting its systems, encrypting saved card details and limiting who could access them. A business that broke the rules could be fined or lose the right to accept cards.

Online card payments became common across Europe and arrived in Croatia.

## Easy to Accept, Easy to Pay (2010s)

The 2010s made accepting cards simple. Services such as **Stripe** (2011) handled the bank account, the payment gateway and the card data for the shop, in exchange for a small fee on each payment. A shop could now start accepting cards within a day, with far fewer security rules to follow.

Shops that saved card numbers could still lose them in a break-in. **Tokenization** solved this by keeping the real number away from the shop. In its place, the shop received a **token**, a substitute value that worked only for that shop or device. Only the payment service or card network that created the token could match it to the real card, so a stolen token was useless anywhere else.

**Digital wallets** were built on tokens. Apple Pay and Google Pay put the card on the phone, saved as a token instead of the card number. Paying online no longer meant typing card details on a small screen: the customer confirmed with a fingerprint or a face scan, and the phone sent the token. The shop never saw the card number, and the bank knew the real cardholder was paying.

In Croatia, online card payments became common.

## Paying Without Cards (2020s)

**Pay-by-bank** is a growing alternative to cards. The customer approves the payment in their banking app, and the money moves from their account to the shop's account, usually within seconds. With no card network in between, the shop pays no card network fees, and there is no card number to steal.

In the euro area, the European Central Bank is preparing the **digital euro**, a digital form of cash that would be free for everyday payments and accepted online and in shops in every euro country. Above all, it would be European, reducing the reliance on Visa and Mastercard, both American companies.
